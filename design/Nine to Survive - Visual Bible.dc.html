01 — ART DIRECTION

The whole thing is a diorama of quiet corporate dread.

We are not making a dashboard. We are building a tiny, believable office you can look into — warm paper light, real desks with real clutter, small people with big silhouettes, and a UI that gets out of the way. The joke is never loud. The world does the comedy; the interface just keeps score.

03 — DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

Seven rules we don't break.

01
The world does the comedy.

Jokes live in objects and body language, not in copy that shouts. If it's funny, it's because a desk told the truth.

02
The office dominates. UI disappears.

The camera looks into a tiny living office. HUD floats at the edges, beautiful but subordinate. You're never "operating software."

03
Nothing is ever fully still.

Small movements only — breathing, glow, steam, a chair swivel. The world is alive but calm. Stillness reads as broken.

04
Read in one second.

Every meter, character, and threat is legible at a glance and from a distance. Clarity over decoration, always.

05
Every object has a backstory.

No filler props. A protein bar, a 19-year award, a cable nest — each says who sits there before they open their mouth.

06
Punch up, never down.

We mock the company, the incentives, the fake language. Never the person suffering under them. Kayla's arc is the reference.

07
Restraint is the whole aesthetic.

One accent does the shouting. One texture, one light, two type families. The discipline is what makes it look expensive.

06 — LIGHTING PRINCIPLES

Two lights fight for the room.

A warm low sun from the window wants to make it beautiful. The overhead fluorescent wants to make it a workplace. Everywhere they meet is the mood of the game — and the monitor glow is the third, colder light nobody chose.

Key · 5:01 sun
Low, warm, from the window wall. Long shadows raking across the floor. This is the beauty light.
Fill · fluorescent
Flat, cool, overhead. Kills the shadows the sun makes. Reads as "at work." Slight, honest flicker.
Accent · monitor glow
Cold blue kick under chins and on desks. The only light that never sleeps. Breathes slowly.
SHADOW RULE

One soft contact shadow per object, warm-tinted (never grey), offset toward the window. Ambient occlusion in the seams where desk meets floor. Nothing casts a hard black shadow — this is a warm room, not a noir.

MOOD DIAL

As Heat rises, the warm sun drains and the fluorescent takes over — the whole floor cools and flattens. Winning a day warms it back. Light is a silent meter.

15 — THE CRITIQUE · PHASE 3 OPENS HERE

What still smells like a machine made it.

Phases 1–2 built a competent identity. Competent is the enemy. Before adding anything, here is the honest audit of everything in this bible that would stop a stranger from believing a studio made it — each weakness paired with the fix that the following sections deliver.

WEAKNESS 01
Grid-perfect alignment betrays the algorithm.

Every prop sits square, every desk centers its monitor, every card shares one border radius. Humans don't place things this way; twenty years of an office definitely doesn't. Perfection reads as generated.

THE FIX

An imperfection budget: every placed object gets 1–4° of rotation, a few px of drift off its "correct" spot, and occasional overlap. Nothing hand-placed is ever axis-aligned. Chairs return to desks at wrong angles. One ceiling tile is discolored. The rule is enforced in §17's sediment catalog.

WEAKNESS 02
The palette is tasteful but unownable.

Warm cream + muted accents is pleasant — and forgettable. Firewatch owns an orange. Untitled Goose owns a green. Right now no single frame of this game is identifiable at thumbnail size.

THE FIX

Commit to the double key-light as THE signature: 5pm honey-gold flooding one side of every frame, monitor-cyan pushing back from the other. Gold vs. screen-glow — freedom vs. the job — in every single shot, menus included. That collision is the game's face.

WEAKNESS 03
Characters are portraits, not actors.

Everyone faces front, symmetrical, weightless — an avatar system, not a cast. No hips, no hands doing anything, no one leaning on furniture, no clothing responding to a body. The silhouettes pass the black test but fail the life test.

THE FIX

Character Language v3 (below): weight and contrapposto as defaults, 3/4 stance, working hands, per-person walk cycles, a five-mood expression matrix, and the rule that idle means doing something — nobody in this office ever just stands.

WEAKNESS 04
The office has props, not history.

The desks now say who sits there today. Nothing says what happened here in 2019. No abandoned desk, no taped-over sign, no repaired carpet seam, no farewell card still pinned up. A building with no scars is a render, not a place.

THE FIX

The twenty-year sediment rule: every room carries at least three artifacts older than every current employee. §17 catalogs 120+ of them, §16 gives every room in the building its own architecture and scar tissue.

WEAKNESS 05
Motion is a list of loops, not an ecosystem.

Steam rises and monitors glow, forever, independently. Nothing causes anything. Real ambient life is chains: the microwave dings and someone gets up. Without cause and effect the motion reads as screensaver.

THE FIX

The ambient life system (§19): four frequency layers — constant loops, minute-scale events, once-a-day moments, and chains where one behavior triggers the next. Ninety catalogued behaviors, all small, none synchronized.

WEAKNESS 06
Squint and it all turns to mush.

Every desk cluster shares the same mid-value wood-on-tan. There's no value structure — no dark anchors, no pools of light leading the eye. Density without hierarchy is noise, and we are about to add a LOT of density.

THE FIX

Value scripting per room: each space gets one dark anchor (the fridge, the server rack, the CEO's desk), one light pool (window, lamp, screen), and mid-tones everywhere else. Clutter lives in the mid-band so it rewards zooming without shouting. §16 assigns anchors room by room.

WEAKNESS 07
Screenshots show a place, never a moment.

Every frame so far is establishing-shot energy. Nobody would stop scrolling and ask "wait — what happened there?" A Steam page needs frozen stories: a firing mid-escort, a birthday nobody attended, one lamp on at 8pm.

THE FIX

Twenty screenshot briefs (§22) — each a story a viewer reconstructs in two seconds, none about mechanics. These are the marketing shots, the wallpapers, and the tone bible in one.

WEAKNESS 08
The HUD is still, faintly, a website.

Uniform rounded rectangles with drop shadows — the vocabulary of web cards. Premium games build HUDs from bespoke shapes and diegetic textures: badges, tabs, receipts, punch-cards.

THE FIX

Rebuild HUD chips as office objects: the escape fund is a punch-card, receipts are a paper-clipped stack, heat tiles are security badges, the objective is a sticky note in the corner — printed textures, slightly crooked, never one shared radius.

CHARACTER LANGUAGE V3 — FROM AVATAR TO ACTOR

Weight, hands, and never standing still.

Weight & balance

Default stance is contrapposto — hip cocked, weight on one leg, shoulder line countering. Perfectly vertical spines are reserved for the Boss and for fear.

3/4 by default

Bodies face 3/4 to camera; heads turn independently toward whatever they're reacting to. Front-facing symmetry only for the deliberate deadpan-to-camera beat.

Hands that work

Mitten-plus-thumb with two knuckle creases. Hands are always occupied: mug, phone, binder, pocket, drumming the desk. An empty open hand is a story beat, not a default.

Clothing folds

Exactly three fold lines per garment — elbow, waist, knee — plus one silhouette-breaker (untucked shirt-tail, rolled sleeve, lanyard swing). More reads busy; fewer reads inflatable.

Walk cycles are casting

Brad bounces on his toes. Dennis shuffles, binder first. The Boss marches from the sternum. Kayla drifts along walls. Marcus ambles. Priya walks 8% faster than everyone. You can name anyone from their gait alone, blurred, at distance.

Idle = doing

23 — THE LIVING OFFICE · PHASE 4 OPENS HERE

The office does not wait for you.

Phases 1–3 made the office look alive. Phase 4 makes it be alive. The design shift is one sentence: every employee is running their own playthrough, and yours is not the main one. You are a coworker in nineteen other people's careers — they arrive late, skip meetings, fall out, fall in love, spiral, recover, and quit whether or not you are watching.

Everyone has a day you never see all of.

Things happen off-screen — and leave evidence.

You return from the kitchen and two people who ate together for years now sit apart. Rachel's laugh is missing from the 10am cluster. There's cake in the war room you weren't invited to. The player reconstructs the office's private life from residue, the way real coworkers do.

The do-nothing test.

Design gate: park the camera and touch nothing for three in-game days. If the office isn't visibly different — a friendship formed, someone crying in the stairwell, a promotion, a resignation letter drafted and deleted — the simulation has failed. The player must always be able to say "I watched a whole story happen and I wasn't in it."

You are weather to them, too.

The cast reacts to your patterns: come in early three days running and Marcus asks what's wrong; skip the lunch table twice and the lunch table discusses you. The office watches back. That symmetry is what makes it a place instead of a stage.

24 — SOCIAL SIMULATION · SIXTEEN INVISIBLE DIALS

No stat sheets. Only symptoms.

Every employee carries sixteen hidden traits. The player never sees a number, a bar, or a tooltip — only behavior. Each trait below is defined by its symptoms: what you'd notice across a week of paying attention. Reading people is the skill the game teaches.

Ambition
Sits nearer the Boss every reorg. Volunteers loudly in meetings, quietly for nothing. Their calendar fills with people above them.
Trust
Measured in screen angle: high trust turns the monitor toward you; low trust minimizes windows when you approach. Lends chargers, or doesn't.
Loyalty
Defends absent people. Repeats your idea in a meeting with your name on it. Loyalty is only visible when it costs something.
Political skill
Never first to speak, never last. Sits where they can see the door. Their name is on nothing that failed. You realize weeks later they arranged everything.
Stress
Shorter walk cycles, harder keystrokes, coffee refills doubling. The stack of unwashed mugs is a live readout everyone can see and no one names.
Burnout
The opposite of stress: everything slows. Camera-off energy in person. The desk plant dies in sync. They stop correcting people who get things wrong.
Confidence
Walks into the war room without notes. Unafraid of silence in conversations. False confidence is louder; real confidence is slower.
Competence
Other people's problems migrate to their desk. The printer works when they leave it. Competence is legible as traffic — who the office walks toward when things break.
Cowardice
Agrees in the meeting, objects in the hallway. Takes the stairs to avoid specific elevators. Their "we should push back" always uses we.
Integrity
Behaves identically whether or not the Boss is on the floor. Returns the extra change to the vending machine. The trait you only discover under pressure — theirs or yours.
Vindictiveness
Never angry in the moment. Three weeks later your meeting room booking mysteriously vanishes. Keeps receipts the way you keep receipts.
Empathy
Notices the crying before HR does. Adjusts their volume near the burned-out. The one who follows you to the stairwell — thirty seconds behind, casually.
Need for approval
Laughs at the Boss's jokes a half-second early. Forwards praise emails. Rewrites work after any raised eyebrow, including imaginary ones.
Risk tolerance
Visible at the margins: who pushes back in all-hands, who expenses the nice lunch, who interviews elsewhere while not unhappy. Cowards call it recklessness; the ambitious call it Tuesday.
Career momentum
The office smells it. Rising people get interrupted less each week; falling people find meetings rescheduled without them. Momentum is other people's behavior, not yours.
Popularity
Measured in drop-bys per hour and birthday-card signature order. Distinct from respect — Brad is popular; Priya is respected; the game never confuses the two.
The inference rule: a trait may only influence what a character does — posture, schedule, seating, silence — never what a tooltip says. If a player can't discover it by watching, the behavior isn't written strongly enough. Fix the behavior, never add a label.
25 — RELATIONSHIPS · THE TRUE PROGRESSION SYSTEM

Your real inventory is people.

Money buys the exit; relationships decide whether you make it there. Every pair of employees carries four quiet dimensions — warmth (do I like you), respect (do I rate you), obligation (do I owe you), history (what have we survived) — and the interesting relationships are the ones where the four disagree.

Friends
High warmth, any respect. Eat together, cover small absences, know each other's coffee orders. Fragile at promotion boundaries.
Mentors
High respect flowing down, obligation flowing up. Marcus teaches survival; the debt is repaid in loyalty, or — the tragic variant — in surpassing him.
Allies
Respect without warmth required. Built on exchanged favors, tracked precisely by both sides. An ally who stops being useful becomes a Convenience Friend on a delay.
Rivals
High mutual respect, contested territory. Rivals sharpen each other and sit in each other's sightlines. The healthiest relationship in the building, weirdly.
Enemies
Rivalry plus a memory that won't fade. Enemies don't compete — they wait. Visible as perfect politeness with zero eye contact.
Work spouses
Maximum warmth and history, zero romance, total shorthand. Finish each other's status updates. When one leaves, the other's performance dips for a month.
Convenience friends
Warmth exactly proportional to usefulness: the person you like because they sit near the printer. Evaporates on desk moves; neither party mourns.
Temporary alliances
Formed against a shared threat — a bad reorg, a new consultant, Adam. Dissolves the day the threat does; both parties pretend it never happened.
Secret dislike
Warmth performed, not felt. Readable only in micro-behavior: the half-second delay before laughing, the birthday card signed last, smallest.
Mutual use
Both know. Neither says. Brad and Greg trade visibility like currency, each certain he's the one exploiting the other. Both right.
Factions
Cliques with a political goal: the Old Guard protecting the way things were, the Ambition Axis racing for the next org chart. Departments are factions with budgets.
The one-way street
Every relationship is asymmetric under the hood. Ethan considers you his mentor; you consider him Tuesday. The game's saddest and funniest stories live in that gap.
Evolution law: no relationship is static for more than three days. Warmth drifts with proximity and shared meals; respect moves on witnessed competence; obligation ticks with favors and expires loudly when unpaid; history only accumulates. Every scene between two characters ends with at least one dimension nudged — the office is a graph that never stops rebalancing.

28 — MEMORIES · THE LONG GAME

The office forgives on a schedule. Some things never clear.

Every character keeps a ledger of what was done to them and for them — who helped, who embarrassed, who covered, who took credit, who apologized, who lied. Memory is what turns a simulation into a biography: today's kindness prices next month's favor.

Decay rates are personality.

Small slights fade in days; favors fade slower than debts (everyone remembers what they're owed); public embarrassment fades by season. But each character's forgiveness curve is a trait: Marcus forgets by Friday, Meredith files, Dennis laminates. Vindictiveness is just a decay rate of zero.

The unfading four.

Betrayal during promotion season. Blame taken for someone else. Public defense when it was dangerous. Credit stolen and never returned. These write to permanent memory, survive reorgs and years, and re-surface at exactly the worst — or best — moment.

Memory is visible as behavior drift.

Nobody announces a grudge. The lunch invitation stops. The screen angles away. The "quick question" goes to someone else now. Players discover what a character remembers the same way you discover it at a real job: by noticing what changed.

Apologies are transactions with interest.

A real apology converts a grudge into a debt of goodwill — but only once per relationship; the second apology for the same sin compounds it. Lies discovered retroactively re-price every memory involving the liar. History is the only dimension that can be rewritten, and only downward.

Story law: long-term stories are memories colliding — the intern you helped in week one is the manager deciding your fate in week three. Every run's climax should trace to a ledger entry the player half-forgot making.

31 — WHY PEOPLE WILL TALK ABOUT THIS GAME · THE CLOSING ARGUMENT

Because they didn't play it. They worked there.

People retell Sims and RimWorld and Crusader Kings stories for one reason: the game didn't tell them a story — it let one happen to them, and they were the only witness. A scripted scene belongs to everyone who bought the game. "My best ally sold me out in calibration week" belongs to one player, forever, and humans cannot hold a story that is theirs alone without telling it.

Nine to Survive has an advantage none of those games have: everyone has already worked here. Nobody has actually governed a medieval dynasty. But everyone has watched a Brad present their work, timed a walk to the kitchen to avoid a Meredith, loved a Marcus, been an Ethan. The simulation doesn't ask players to imagine a world — it asks them to recognize one. Every emergent story lands twice: once as a game event, once as a memory of a real Tuesday.

The specificity is the flavor. Not "a coworker betrayed me" but "Rachel, who signed my birthday card largest, flagged my expense report the week of calibration." Systems that track warmth, debts, and grudges produce that texture automatically — and texture is what survives five years of retelling. Details are the proof the story really happened, and this office manufactures details the way other games manufacture loot.

And the stories are safe. Real office politics wound; this office is a terrarium where the layoff isn't yours, the firing is watchable, the betrayal is survivable, and you can finally take the screenshot. Players get to feel the full weight of corporate life with the one thing real employment never offers: an exit door with a number on it, and the certainty that the door opens.

So the five-years-later conversation isn't "remember that boss fight." It's two people at a real office kitchen, one saying "this is such a Dennis situation," and both knowing exactly what that means. The game's characters become shorthand for real life. That is the deepest form of word of mouth that exists — when your cast escapes the game and gets hired, informally, by reality.

The player should feel like they escaped a real company.
